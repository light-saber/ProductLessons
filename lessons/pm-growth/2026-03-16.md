# Daily PM Lesson — March 16, 2026

**Category:** Organizational Design

---

## Hook

Here's a dirty secret about org design: **the org chart is the product architecture.** Conway's Law isn't a suggestion — it's gravity. If your teams are structured wrong, it doesn't matter how brilliant your strategy is. The product will mirror the dysfunction. So why do most PMs treat org design as "someone else's problem"?

---

## Concept: Own the Topology, Own the Outcomes

Marty Cagan argues that the **head of product and head of engineering should co-own team topology** — it's not an HR exercise, it's a product decision. Every team boundary you draw determines what gets built fast and what gets stuck in cross-team coordination hell.

Will Larson (author of *An Elegant Puzzle*) offers a brutally practical framework for sizing:

- **6-8 engineers per team** in steady state. Below 4, the manager becomes a part-time tech lead with stunted growth. Above 8, they become a firefighting coach with no time to invest.
- **4-6 managers per director.** Fewer leads to meddling. More leads to pure escalation-routing.
- **Never create empty teams.** To spin up a new team, grow an existing one to 8-10, then bud it into two teams of 4-5.

Cagan layers on the *why*: teams should be **empowered, not merely staffed.** An empowered team gets a *problem to solve*, not a feature to build. They're cross-functional (PM + designer + engineers), outcome-accountable, and autonomous in discovery. A "delivery team" that just executes a roadmap is an org design failure, no matter how perfectly sized.

The trap at large companies like Walmart? You optimize for throughput (ship more features) instead of outcomes (move the metric). The org chart reinforces this — teams organized around components or projects rather than customer problems.

---

## Apply It

**Today's exercise:** Pick one team you work closely with. Map it against these questions:

1. **What problem does this team own?** If the answer is "they build X feature" instead of "they solve Y customer problem" — that's a topology smell.
2. **How many cross-team dependencies does their current initiative have?** More than 2 blocking dependencies = the team boundaries are drawn wrong.
3. **Is the team sized for sustainability?** Count the humans. Check Larson's 6-8 range.

Write down one concrete re-topology suggestion you'd pitch to your engineering counterpart. Even if you never pitch it — the muscle of *thinking in team shapes* is what separates Principal PMs from Directors.

---

## Mini Challenge

**Scenario:** Your VP asks you to "just add a team" for a new grocery delivery initiative. You currently have a 12-person commerce team. What do you do?

- (a) Create a new 4-person team from scratch and hire
- (b) Split the 12-person team into a 7-person commerce team and a 5-person grocery delivery team
- (c) Keep the 12-person team and add the initiative to their backlog
- (d) Something else?

Think through tradeoffs for each. There's no single right answer, but **(b)** is closest to Larson's budding model — and it gives the new team institutional context instead of starting cold.
