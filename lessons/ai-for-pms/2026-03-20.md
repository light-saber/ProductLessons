# AI Safety, Alignment & Hallucinations: The PM's Trust Problem

**Topic:** AI Safety, Alignment & Hallucinations  
**Date:** 2026-03-20  
**Series:** AI for PMs — Day 8

---

## 1. Technical Explanation

**Hallucination** is when an LLM generates plausible-sounding but factually incorrect output. It's not a bug — it's a structural property of next-token prediction. The model doesn't "know" facts; it predicts probable text.

Current hallucination rates (2026): Claude Sonnet ~3% on summarization, GPT-5.2 ~8-12%, open-source models 15-30%. Reasoning models paradoxically hallucinate *more* on factual benchmarks — OpenAI's o3 hit 33% on PersonQA — because deeper reasoning can generate more confident-sounding wrong answers.

**Alignment** ensures the model follows intended behavior (helpful, harmless, honest). Techniques include RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback), Constitutional AI (Anthropic's approach — the model critiques its own outputs against principles), and deliberative alignment (o1-style models reason about safety policies in their chain-of-thought).

**Grounding** is the practical antidote: RAG reduces hallucinations by ~71%, tool-calling routes factual queries to verified systems, and citation enforcement forces the model to show its work. Benchmarks like Vectara, FACTS, and HalluHard measure these properties systematically.

---

## 2. Simple Explanation

Imagine hiring a brilliant consultant who's read millions of documents but has no way to look anything up in real-time. When you ask a question, they don't say "I don't know" — they give you their best guess with total confidence. That's hallucination.

**Alignment** is like training that consultant to follow company policy — don't be rude, don't make stuff up, admit uncertainty. **Grounding** is giving them access to the actual company database so they can check facts instead of guessing.

The key insight: you can't fully eliminate hallucinations. You design *systems* that catch them — citations, confidence scores, human review for high-stakes decisions. The best AI products treat the model as one component in a trust pipeline, not the final authority.

---

## 3. Real Example

Walmart's product Q&A system faces this directly. If a customer asks "Is this TV compatible with my wall mount?", an ungrounded LLM might confidently say "yes" based on pattern matching. A hallucination here means a returned product and an angry customer.

The PM decision: instead of deploying a raw LLM, the team implements RAG against structured product specs, requires the model to cite specific attributes (VESA pattern, weight limits), and adds a confidence threshold — below 80%, the response becomes "Check the product specifications" with a link. This turns a ~12% hallucination rate into a near-zero customer-facing error rate. The PM traded some response coverage for trust.

---

## 4. Key Takeaways

- **Hallucination is structural**, not a bug to be patched — plan for it in your product architecture
- **Reasoning ≠ accuracy**: more sophisticated models can hallucinate more confidently on factual tasks
- **RAG + citations + tool-calling** are your grounding stack — they reduce hallucination rates by 50-70%
- **Design for graceful failure**: confidence thresholds, "I don't know" responses, and human escalation paths matter more than model selection
- **Measure it**: use hallucination benchmarks (Vectara, FACTS) in your eval pipeline — don't ship without knowing your rate
