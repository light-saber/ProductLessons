# Fine-tuning vs Prompt Engineering vs RAG: The PM's Decision Framework

**Topic:** Fine-tuning vs Prompt Engineering vs RAG  
**Date:** 2026-03-18

---

## 1. Technical Explanation

As a PM, you have three levers to customize LLM behavior — each with different cost, latency, and effort profiles.

**Prompt Engineering** is the cheapest and fastest. You craft instructions, examples, and constraints directly in the prompt. Zero infrastructure cost, instant iteration. OpenAI's optimization docs recommend starting here — write evals first, then iterate on prompts. Works for 80%+ of use cases. Cost: just your token spend per call (~$0.01-$3 per request depending on model).

**RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)** injects external knowledge at query time. Your system retrieves relevant documents from a vector database and stuffs them into the context window. Adds ~200-500ms latency per retrieval step. Infrastructure cost: embedding pipeline + vector DB (Pinecone, Weaviate). Best when your data changes frequently — product catalogs, policy docs, inventory.

**Fine-tuning** retrains model weights on your domain data. OpenAI charges ~$8/1M training tokens for GPT-4o mini. Takes hours to days. Creates a specialized model that's faster at inference (shorter prompts needed) but frozen at training time. Best for consistent style/format or proprietary domain language — not for injecting facts.

The hierarchy: **prompt engineering first → RAG for knowledge → fine-tuning for behavior.**

## 2. Simple Explanation

Think of it like training a new employee:

**Prompt engineering** = giving them detailed written instructions for each task. "Here's exactly how to handle this customer complaint." Quick, flexible, but you repeat yourself every time.

**RAG** = giving them access to a company knowledge base. "Look up the return policy before answering." They always have current info, but it takes a moment to search.

**Fine-tuning** = sending them through a specialized training program. They internalize your company's tone, jargon, and workflows. Expensive upfront, but they just *get it* afterwards. The downside? Their training becomes outdated — you can't easily update what they learned.

Most teams should try instructions first, add a knowledge base second, and only invest in training programs when the first two aren't enough.

## 3. Real Example

**Walmart's product description generator:** Imagine building an AI tool that writes product listings for walmart.com. You'd start with **prompt engineering** — "Write a 50-word product description in Walmart's brand voice, emphasizing value." That gets you 70% there.

Next, you'd add **RAG** — retrieve the product's specs, pricing, competitor descriptions, and category guidelines from your catalog database. Now the AI writes accurate, current descriptions.

Only if thousands of listings still sound "off-brand" would you **fine-tune** on 10,000 approved Walmart product descriptions to nail the exact tone. That fine-tuning costs maybe $50-100 and saves you from repeating style instructions in every prompt — reducing per-call token costs by 30-40%.

The PM decision: fine-tuning was justified only after measuring that prompt engineering + RAG left a measurable quality gap on evals.

## 4. Key Takeaways

- **Start with prompt engineering** — it's free, instant, and solves most problems. Don't skip to fine-tuning.
- **Use RAG when the model needs current or private data** — product catalogs, docs, policies. Facts belong in retrieval, not in model weights.
- **Fine-tune for behavior, not knowledge** — tone, format, domain jargon. Fine-tuned models go stale; RAG stays current.
- **The cost ladder matters:** prompt engineering (~$0) → RAG (~$500-2K/mo infra) → fine-tuning (~$50-500 per training run + ongoing retraining).
- **Always build evals before choosing your approach** — you can't pick the right lever if you can't measure the gap.
